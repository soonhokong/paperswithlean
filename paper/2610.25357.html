Studying proof data with proof theory: the case of AI-generated mathematics

Safal Raman Aryal

math.LO Oct 18, 2026 · v1 cs.LO

TL;DR
Compiles AI-written Lean corpora and human projects against a fixed Mathlib version and measures the proof terms Lean checks, comparing their compression against cut-free forms.
Abstract
I present a method for analyzing large collections of formally verified mathematical proofs (e.g. OpenAI's) grounded in some historical and philosophical musings on mathematics in the age of AI. Many of the quantities involved, such as the size of a proof term or the number of redundant steps, are already computed by work on improving automated provers, where they serve as targets. My aim is to turn them into measurements of proofs themselves: compared against a reference library and against each proof's own cut-free form, checked against theorems they must obey, and controlled for how the proofs were compiled. With these instruments, I measure how (much) machine-generated mathematics diverges from human mathematics, and find that AI-written formal proofs realize far less of the logical compression available to them than human mathematicians do (31% against 73% in mathlib, and 55% against 86% on proofs that call no automation). These figures are broadly in line with reports from those who build provers and proof-improvement tools that AI-written proofs are often longer than they need to be.
Problem

Large collections of AI-generated formal proofs, such as OpenAI's mathematics library, are now available, and it is unclear how they differ structurally from human-written formal mathematics. Quantities like proof-term size and redundant steps are usually treated as optimization targets for provers rather than as measurements of the proofs themselves.

Approach

96 projects from OpenAI's AI-written library, three smaller AI-written corpora, and two human research projects were compiled against the same Mathlib commit. The resulting kernel-checked proof terms were measured. Each proof is compared against Mathlib as a human reference and against its own cut-free form, checked against theorems the measures must obey, and controlled for compilation choices.

Results

AI-written proofs realize far less of the available logical compression than human proofs: 31% versus 73% for Mathlib, and 55% versus 86% on proofs that call no automation. In all 17 shared mathematical areas, OpenAI's median project costs more than Mathlib's.
