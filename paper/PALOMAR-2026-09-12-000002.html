A Tight Upper Bound for Fully Dynamic Matching on the Line

Yash Kanoria

TL;DR
Lean formalization, machine-checked by the Palomar registry, of a tight log(m)/m upper bound for one-dimensional fully dynamic spatial matching.
Abstract
There are `m` labeled supplies in `[0,1]`. In each period, an independent uniform demand arrives; an online policy selects one live supply, pays the distance between them, and replaces that supply by an independent uniform point. This is the one-dimensional fully dynamic model studied in Kanoria's [*Dynamic spatial matching*](https://doi.org/10.1214/25-AAP2154). That paper established a lower bound of order `log(m)/m` and an upper bound of order `log(m)^2/m`, and posed closing this logarithmic gap as an open problem. We construct a policy and show that its performance matches the prior known lower bound up to a universal constant factor. Specifically, for every `m >= 2`, from every fixed initial inventory, its root-mean-square one-period cost has limsup at most `C log(m+1)/m`, with `C = 72000/log(2)`. With a scheduled `m`-period replacement phase, its expected average cost, including that phase, has the same bound for every total horizon `N >= 2m^2`. The policy assigns deletion probabilities recursively on a dyadic tree and implements them by quantile transport. Its analysis combines a harmonic inventory potential with a quadratic Bellman inequality. Tree symmetry cancels interactions between spatial scales, while the Bellman inequality controls both transport discrepancy and the curvature loss in the potential drift.

Problem

In the one-dimensional fully dynamic matching model, m supplies sit in [0,1]. Each period a uniform demand arrives and is matched to a supply at distance cost, and that supply is then replaced by a fresh uniform point. Kanoria left a logarithmic gap between the known lower bound log(m)/m and upper bound log(m)^2/m as an open problem.

Approach

The authors build a policy that assigns deletion probabilities recursively on a dyadic tree and implements them by quantile transport. The analysis combines a harmonic inventory potential with a quadratic Bellman inequality. Tree symmetry cancels interactions between spatial scales, and the Bellman inequality controls both transport discrepancy and curvature loss in the potential drift. The proofs are formalized in Lean and machine-checked by the Palomar registry.

Results

For every m >= 2 and every fixed initial inventory, the root-mean-square one-period cost has limsup at most C log(m+1)/m, with C = 72000/log 2. This matches the lower bound up to a constant factor. With a scheduled m-period replacement phase, the expected average cost satisfies the same bound for every horizon N >= 2m^2.
