Abstract
We formalize the boundary of naturality in the labelled conversion calculus of A Theory of a Two-Dimensional Typed Lambda Calculus. Canonical evaluation of inductively presented homotopies constructs naturality squares, and transport along finite presentations preserves two-paths and recovers the naturality boundary. In contrast, an explicit pointwise family on Fin 3 admits no naturality two-cell or finite pasting: a six-element dihedral-group invariant separates its two boundary paths while respecting every declared two-cell generator. This independently reconstructed refutation is classical, not a verification of the manuscript's claimed constructive Idris proof. Supporting results distinguish beta and eta evidence by parity and give a standalone typed de Bruijn syntax with exact raw-tree representation adequacy and a parity-preserving conversion interpretation. The result concerns this declared evidence calculus, not normalization, full abstraction, or a completeness theorem for lambda calculus.

Problem

Within a labelled conversion calculus for a two-dimensional typed lambda calculus, the work asks where naturality of homotopies holds and whether it can hold for arbitrary pointwise families.

Approach

Canonical evaluation of inductively presented homotopies is formalized to construct naturality squares, and transport along finite presentations is shown to preserve two-paths. A counterexample on Fin 3 is built, with a six-element dihedral-group invariant separating its boundary paths while respecting every declared two-cell generator. Supporting developments separate beta and eta evidence by parity and give a typed de Bruijn syntax with raw-tree representation adequacy.

Results

Naturality holds for inductively presented homotopies, but the explicit pointwise family admits no naturality two-cell or finite pasting. The refutation is reconstructed classically and does not verify the manuscript's claimed constructive Idris proof. The results cover only this evidence calculus, not normalization, full abstraction, or completeness.
