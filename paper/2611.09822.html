Discovering formally verified quantum programs with AI

TL;DR
Builds a Lean/Mathlib library with a classical-quantum Hoare logic and resource counting, in which LLM agents synthesize and verify quantum programs against Lean specifications.
Abstract
The timeline by which quantum computers can solve breakthrough problems in science depends on improved quantum algorithms as much as on better hardware. Such algorithmic advances are typically achieved through many years of expert human collaboration. In mathematics and computer science, large language models have accelerated progress through automated discovery loops. However, quantum algorithms can only be evaluated by classical computers on restricted or small instances. Here we demonstrate automated discovery of quantum programs beyond these regimes including order-of-magnitude improvements on scientific applications. To achieve this, we build a quantum program library in the Lean theorem prover in which Gemini 4 agents synthesize novel programs against formal problem specifications and verify their correctness and cost. Agent teams collaborate, sharing results and knowledge through wikis and discussing ideas at organized conferences. Our results improve on prior baselines for parallel search (up to 2.57x), quantum arithmetic (290x), and simulations relevant to fusion, photochemistry and drug-metabolism by 12x, 8x and 2.5x respectively. Combining human and artificial intelligence at scale, under the rigor of mathematical proof, lets researchers design algorithms that today's classical and quantum computers cannot test, shortening the path to practical quantum computation.
Problem

Progress on quantum algorithms is slow and depends on expert humans. Candidate algorithms can only be checked by classical simulation on small instances, which limits automated discovery at application scale.

Approach

The authors build a Lean library on top of Mathlib and Lean-QuantumInfo. It provides a classical-quantum programming language, a quantum Hoare logic based on weakest preconditions, and bounds on expected and worst-case resource costs such as T-count and qubit count. Problems are posed as Lean files containing sorry placeholders for the program, its cost bound, and the proofs. Teams of Gemini agents fill these in and share results through wikis and organized conferences, and an external verifier using Lean Comparator checks each submission against the frozen specification.

Results

Verified improvements over prior baselines: up to 2.55x fewer expected oracle rounds for parallel Grover search and 290x lower spacetime cost for fixed-point arcsin. Simulations for fusion stopping power, photochemistry dynamics and cytochrome P450 improve by 10.9x, 8.08x and 2.49x respectively.
