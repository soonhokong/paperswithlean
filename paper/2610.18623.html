Minimax rates for pointwise CATE estimation: the role of design smoothness

Yuxuan Hou, Zhenghan Li

math.ST Oct 13, 2026 · v1 cs.LG stat.ML
formalization probability
Read PDF arXiv abstract Code
TL;DR
The proofs of the minimax upper and lower bounds for pointwise CATE estimation are provided as Lean 4 code in a public GitHub repository.
Abstract
We study the role of design smoothness in the minimax estimation of conditional average treatment effects at a fixed covariate point. For design densities bounded above and away from zero, without further regularity, we derive upper and lower risk bounds with an arbitrarily small gap in the convergence-rate exponent. Under uniform design, we obtain matching upper and lower bounds at interior points in the regime of low nuisance smoothness. These results show that rough covariate designs can lead to slower rates than uniform designs, even under the same smoothness assumptions on the treatment effect and nuisance functions, highlighting the role of the covariate distribution in pointwise causal estimation.
Problem

Earlier minimax lower bounds for pointwise conditional average treatment effect (CATE) estimation over Hölder classes used design densities that vanish on parts of the domain. It was open whether the same rates hold under uniform design, and what is attainable when the density is only bounded above and below.

Approach

For rough designs, the authors build an estimator from weighted contrasts of nearby observations. These are polynomial stencils with null-vector weights that cancel low-order polynomials without estimating the density. They pair it with a minimax lower bound. For uniform design, they analyze a second-order local polynomial R-learner with a projection correction and prove a matching lower bound at interior points. Lean 4 code for the proofs is released on GitHub.

Results

Under rough design, the upper and lower risk bounds differ by an arbitrarily small gap in the rate exponent. Under uniform design, the bounds match at interior points when nuisance smoothness is low. Rough covariate designs can therefore yield slower rates than uniform designs under identical smoothness assumptions.
