TrustBound: Mechanized Completeness for Payment Epochs Initiated by AI Agents

Sandip Nandi, Aishwarya Bose, Apurba Bose, Soovadeep Bakshi

cs.CR Nov 14, 2026 · v1 cs.LO

TL;DR
Lean 4 models of a payment admission discipline and provider contract, with mechanized closure and refinement theorems; executed service histories are checked against the model in Lean.
Abstract
Payment operations requested by an AI agent may remain executable, or their outcomes unresolved, after the agent has stopped. We study when a trusted payment service can account for every payment in an epoch and exclude further payments under earlier authority. We give a Lean model in which operations are recorded before they receive execution authority and both actions are serialized with an admission fence. We prove that fencing fixes a journal that covers every modeled effect and outstanding right; that discharging those rights prevents further effects; and that truthful outcomes for the recorded identities then give exact accounting. A provider-contract model, mapped to the admission model, makes created payment objects inert until registered. Premise-removal mutations confirm that each key premise is needed. We connect the models to an executable service: six service histories are checked against the model in Lean, and bounded schedules, scripted clients and concurrent workers exercise its reconciliation. The concurrent experiments uncovered a conflict caused by reordered provider reads, which serializing retrieval with journal updates removes in all tested cases. The result separates two obligations that are easily conflated in practice - preventing future payments and accounting for past ones - and states precisely which service guarantees establish each.
Problem

Payments requested by AI agents may still execute, or have unknown outcomes, after the agent is stopped. The question is what a trusted payment service must guarantee to exclude further payments under earlier authority and to account exactly for every payment in an epoch.

Approach

An admission model (journal, issued identities, remaining rights, effects, fence flag) is defined in Lean 4, and a closure theorem is proved: fencing fixes a journal covering all effects and rights, discharging the rights prevents further effects, and truthful outcomes give exact accounting. A provider-contract model inspired by Stripe manual capture is proved in Lean to refine the admission model. Premise-removal mutations test that each premise is needed. A Python/SQLite service is built, and its recorded histories are projected into model transitions and checked in Lean.

Results

All 14 Lean results check using only the standard axioms, and all 8 premise-removal mutations are rejected. Six service histories (64 transitions) are accepted by Lean. Concurrent testing exposed a conflict from reordered provider reads, which serializing retrieval with journal updates removed in all 84 tested cases.
